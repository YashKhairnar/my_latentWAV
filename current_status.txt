## What you understood correctly

Your component mapping is exactly the sensible one:

| WAV component | Your implementation |
|---|---|
| Action-free future prior \(g\) | NanoWM |
| Inverse dynamics model \(h\) | Pixel IDM |
| Forward world model \(f\) | LeWM |
| Verification score | LeWM prediction–subgoal latent MSE |
| Acquisition | Execute the maximum-disagreement action block |
| Self-improvement data | Real PushT successor transition |

The ordering is also correct:

```text
current state → plausible subgoals → inferred actions
              → LeWM predictions → maximum disagreement
              → execute → collect real outcome
```

That is WAV’s goal-oriented verification cycle, and the paper explicitly permits computing disagreement in a continuous representation space such as LeWM’s latent space. [WAV paper §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01985v2#S2)

## The central conceptual correction

A large disagreement does **not yet mean “LeWM is wrong.”** It means:

> NanoWM’s proposed subgoal disagrees with what LeWM predicts under the IDM’s inferred action.

That disagreement can arise from three different causes:

1. LeWM is wrong—the desired WAV signal.
2. NanoWM generated an implausible or poorly conditioned future.
3. The IDM inferred an action that does not actually reach the subgoal.

At present, you have not separated these explanations. Therefore, the score is a **candidate error proxy**, not a demonstrated LeWM error detector.

The WAV paper validates this by measuring Spearman/Kendall rank correlation between verification score and ground-truth prediction error. That experiment is still missing here. [WAV ranking evaluation](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01985v2#Sx13.SS2.SSS3)

## What is still incomplete

1. **NanoWM is not an action-free prior.**  
   The checkpoint has `use_action: true` in [config.yaml](/Users/yash/Desktop/my_latentWAV/checkpoints/nanowm-pusht/config.yaml:8), but you invoke it without actions. The code works, but inference is outside its training condition. This removes one of WAV’s key asymmetries.

2. **The IDM is dense, not sparse.**  
   WAV’s justification depends on the inverse model using a compact action-relevant subset. Your IDM sees the full images and has no learned sparsity mechanism. A vanilla IDM is an ablation in the WAV paper, not the full method.

3. **IDM MSE does not establish reachability.**  
   A normalized validation MSE of `0.09098` is encouraging, but the critical test is: when its predicted five-action block is executed from the source state, does it actually reach the target/subgoal? Your documentation correctly says this remains untested.

4. **There is no random acquisition baseline.**  
   Without equal-budget random candidate selection, the 205-episode improvement cannot be attributed to maximum disagreement. More data alone—or merely the NanoWM/IDM candidate distribution—could explain it.

5. **It is not yet a repeated self-improvement loop.**  
   WAV updates both the forward model and IDM after acquisition and repeats. Your experiments collect with a largely fixed pipeline, update LeWM once, and keep the IDM frozen. This is explicitly still missing in [README.md](/Users/yash/Desktop/my_latentWAV/README.md:416).

6. **Fine-tuning omitted the warm-up/expert replay data.**  
   Training only on narrow WAV rollouts predictably caused forgetting. WAV experiments retain warm-up data while adding acquired data. Your next mixed expert/WAV replay proposal is the correct fix.

7. **Offline evaluation is too short-horizon.**  
   [compare_lewm.py](/Users/yash/Desktop/my_latentWAV/scripts/compare_lewm.py:71) measures teacher-forced next-latent prediction. CEM uses five autoregressive model steps. A candidate may improve one-step MSE while becoming much worse after error compounding—which is exactly what Experiment 09 suggests.

8. **Full research conclusions need multiple seeds.**  
   The WAV paper averages its main comparisons across several seeds. You currently have one trained candidate and one 50-pair evaluation set.

## How to interpret your results

The 205-episode result establishes:

> Fine-tuning improved one-step latent prediction on held-out episodes drawn from the same WAV acquisition distribution.

It does **not** establish that:

- WAV selection was better than random;
- the verifier ranks real LeWM errors correctly;
- LeWM improved generally;
- planning improved.

Your control result strongly establishes that this particular candidate is worse for planning:

- Expert: 64% success
- Fine-tuned: 28%
- Expert-only successes: 22
- Candidate-only successes: 4

From the saved paired outcomes, the exact paired test gives approximately `p = 0.00053`. Rejecting that checkpoint was therefore the right decision—not an overreaction. See [Experiment 09](/Users/yash/Desktop/my_latentWAV/docs/experiments/experiment-09/README.md:25).

This does not refute WAV. It rejects this training recipe and checkpoint.

One additional warning: the official LeWM paper reports about `96%` PushT success using the same headline CEM settings, whereas your baseline reaches `64%`. [LeWM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19312v3), [official evaluation configuration](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mengarr/lewm/main/config/eval/pusht.yaml). Different sampled goals, seeds, checkpoint versions, or evaluator details may explain this, but absolute planning conclusions should wait until the expert checkpoint reproduces the official protocol more closely.

## The most important next experiment

Before collecting more data or training a larger model, validate the verifier:

1. At many saved PushT states, generate the same \(K\) NanoWM candidates.
2. Infer all \(K\) IDM action blocks.
3. From cloned simulator states, execute every candidate block.
4. For every candidate, record:
   - WAV score: predicted LeWM latent vs subgoal latent;
   - true LeWM error: predicted LeWM latent vs real resulting latent;
   - reachability error: subgoal latent vs real resulting latent.
5. Measure Spearman and Kendall correlation between WAV score and true LeWM error.
6. Compare selection by:
   - random candidate;
   - maximum WAV score;
   - Oracle true error.

If maximum WAV score does not reliably rank true errors, further fine-tuning will not test the WAV hypothesis—it will mostly train on whatever failures NanoWM and the IDM happen to produce.

After that, implement mixed expert/WAV replay, repeated rounds, IDM updates, multi-horizon evaluation, and at least three independent seeds.

## Bottom line

You have built a coherent engineering foundation, and your mental model of WAV is substantially correct. You have also been appropriately conservative about rejecting bad checkpoints.

The scientifically accurate summary today is:

> We implemented a WAV-inspired acquisition pipeline around LeWM on PushT. Fine-tuning improved one-step prediction on the acquired-data distribution but caused forgetting and significantly degraded CEM control. That is a legitimate and useful research result—but it is not yet evidence that WAV successfully improves LeWM.
